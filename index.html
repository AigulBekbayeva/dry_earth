Dry Earth

Three maps about drought: where water is scarce, where it is getting scarcer, and who that hurts most.

Where are the driest places on Earth?

About of the world's land gets less than 250 mm of rain and snow a year, the usual line for a desert. On of it, less than 25 mm falls: the eastern Sahara, the Atacama coast, the Tarim Basin and the high plateau of Antarctica.

      How this map was made

      Precipitation: CRU CL 2.0, a 10-arc-minute (about 18 km) climatology of monthly station records for 1961–1990 (New et al., 2002). It has no data for Antarctica, so that continent is filled from GPCP v2.2 satellite-gauge estimates for 1979–2010 on a much coarser 2.5° grid.

      Aridity index: yearly precipitation divided by reference evapotranspiration, which is computed here with the Hargreaves equation from the same dataset's monthly minimum and maximum temperatures. Classes follow UNEP: below 0.05 hyper-arid, 0.05–0.2 arid, 0.2–0.5 semi-arid, 0.5–0.65 dry sub-humid. Not computed for Antarctica.

      Read with care: a grid cell is an average over roughly 300 km², so single weather stations can be drier or wetter than the map. Where stations are few, as in Greenland, the central Sahara and Tibet, values are interpolated over long distances. The baseline period is decades old; desert margins have shifted a little since.

      Which regions are getting drier?

      By mid-century, climate models agree most firmly on two: the Mediterranean and south-western South America. Southern Africa, southern Australia, Central America and the northern Amazon follow with less agreement. Most other regions gain rainfall, yet all of them warm, and warmer air takes more water back from soil and crops.

        How this map was made

        Data: CMIP6 climate-model output, averaged over the land of each IPCC AR6 reference region and published in the IPCC WGI Interactive Atlas repository (Iturbide et al., 2021). One run per model; models, depending on the pathway.

        Change: average yearly precipitation in 2041–2060, the 20 years centred on 2050, compared with 1995–2014 in the same model. The map shows the median across models. Hatching marks regions where fewer than 80% of models agree on whether rainfall goes up or down.

        Read with care: rainfall is only one side of dryness. Soil moisture also depends on evaporation, which rises with temperature, so a region with a small gain in rain can still end up with drier soils. Regions are large; change inside them is uneven. "Today's rainfall" in the tooltip comes from the W5E5 observational dataset, 1995–2014.

          How this map was made

          Drought frequency: the share of 12-month periods in 1979–2010 when rainfall was below 80% of its long-term average (GPCP v2.2, 2.5° grid), averaged over a country. Grid cells with less than 100 mm a year are left out: where it almost never rains, a shortfall means little.

          Population density (people per km² of land) and employment in agriculture (% of all employment, ILO modelled estimates) are the latest values in the World Bank's World Development Indicators.

          Overlap score: each ingredient is converted to a percentile rank among the rated countries; the score is the geometric mean of the three ranks, times 100. A country has to be high on all three to score high.

          Read with care: this is an index of exposure, not a forecast of famine. It ignores irrigation, rivers fed from abroad, income, and how well a country copes. The rainfall grid is coarse, so countries smaller than 5,000 km² are not rated. National averages hide dry provinces inside large countries. Borders follow Natural Earth and imply no position on disputed territories.

          Data: CRU CL 2.0, GPCP v2.2, IPCC WGI Atlas (CMIP6), World Bank WDI, Natural Earth. Built with MapLibre GL JS. Sources and method are described in the README.
